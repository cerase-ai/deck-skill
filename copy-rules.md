# Writing rules

The rules for every line of the deck: titles, bullets, captions, quotes. When a rule conflicts with what the person explicitly asked for, follow the person.

## 0. Seven procedures

Everything from rule 1 on is a prohibition or a test, and a prohibition needs you to catch yourself while writing the line, convinced the line is good. That is exactly when it fails. These seven change the order of the work instead, so the empty line is never written.

**(a) Write the slide's sentence in full before building the slide.** Then build the slide to carry it. **No sentence, no slide**: if you cannot write what the slide establishes, it has no takeaway, and it is merged into its neighbour or cut. Writing the outline first and the sentences after reverses this: the slot exists, so a line gets invented to fill it.

**(b) A slide may end on its table.** Or on its chart, or its facts. There is no required closing line. A layout that wants a closing sentence on every slide makes you write assertions nobody had: four abstract nouns with no verb under a table that already said it.

**(c) Over the budget, remove a fact, a row or a whole slide. Never shorten a sentence.** Shortening removes information first and rhythm last, because rhythm is what survives rewriting: a sentence cut five times keeps its shape and loses its content. A page that does not fit carries too much; it is not written too long.

**Definitions go last, and a term leaves with its definition.** Under page pressure a definition looks like the cheapest line on the slide, because it carries no number, so it is the first thing the eye offers to cut. Cut it last. And when a term cannot keep its definition, cut the term as well: a word the reader cannot decode is worse than one fact fewer.

**(d) The label test.** Show someone a label and one value under it. They must be able to say what the value asserts. `Stopped? / no` fails: the reader cannot tell whether the line was never stopped, whether stops are out of scope, or whether nobody records them. `Line stopped during the test? / no` passes.

Apply it to **every column header and every first-column row label, on every slide**. A label fixed on one slide survives on the next if only the header was looked at. No other rule reaches a label, because a label cannot be deleted: a table needs its headers, so an empty one survives every other check.

**(e) The decode test.** Every term that names a method, a standard, an article of a rule, a material, an instrument or a unit carries its meaning in the same sentence or the next, the first time it appears. Read the line as the audience: someone who knows their own business but not this field must be able to say what it asserts. `OTIF fell to 91%` fails for a board that does not run logistics; `Orders delivered complete and on the agreed day (OTIF) fell to 91%` passes.

A dense line of jargon passes every other rule: deleting it loses information, it is not a label, and it states a fact. Only this test asks whether the reader can decode it.

**Your own familiarity is not evidence.** By the time you draft, every term of the field is clear to you. Test each one against the brief's two vocabulary lists, and where they do not settle it, define the term.

**A deck in a second language is written again from the facts, not translated.** Translation keeps the source's compression, and a shorthand its readers absorb becomes opaque in the other language. Derive every label again from what a value under it asserts, and run the decode test again in the new language.

**(f) Provenance: every claim carries its source, and ours are marked as ours.** Before a number goes on a slide, name where it came from: **the vendor**, **a rule, a register or a public record**, **a primary source we read**, **our own arithmetic**, **our own judgement**, or **someone's word**. The first three are stated plainly. Our arithmetic and our judgement are stated **and marked as ours inside the sentence** ("our multiplication", "our estimate"), because otherwise an estimate and a published figure read in the same voice. Someone's word is checked or dropped; it never ships.

Never state a cost, a size or a time inside the audience's own trade unless you read it somewhere. They price it for a living, and a wrong figure there costs more than it was worth.

**(g) The actor: every open question, every instruction and every "we" names who acts.** A list of questions with no subject reads as homework: who asks them, of whom, why. If we will get the answer, say so. If it belongs to the reader's own conversation with a supplier, say that, and ask whether they want us to run it.

## 1. A title is the sentence that sums up what matters

A slide's `## ` title states the takeaway, not the topic. The audience reads the title first: if it carries the conclusion, the body backs it up; if it is a label, they read the whole slide to find out what you mean.

There are two ways to get it wrong, and the second is the dangerous one. A label says too little. A slogan says it with rhetoric instead of information, and because it sounds sharper it passes review.

| Label | Slogan | Summary |
|---|---|---|
| "Market data" | "The market waits for no one" | "Demand for refrigerated transport in the region grew 9% a year since 2022" |
| "Q3 results" | "Q3: the quarter that got away" | "Q3 came in 6% under plan, because two large customers ordered late" |
| "Competition" | "One player stands alone" | "We are the only one of the four suppliers delivering next day" |

The right-hand column is longer. That is expected: it carries information instead of emphasis. Never trade a fact for a rhythm.

Two tests, both must pass:
1. **Substitution**: could the title sit unchanged on three decks about three subjects? Then it is a label.
2. **Information**: take the rhetoric away and ask what the reader now knows. If nothing, it is a slogan.

## 2. The conclusion first

The cover and the first content slide already give the audience the conclusion; the rest of the deck proves it. An audience that agrees after three slides stops arguing. An audience that waits for slide 22 argues on slide 4 about evidence it does not yet know matters. This holds for a deck that only informs: its first slide says what the audience will know at the end.

## 3. One idea per slide

If this slide were the only one the audience saw, what would they take away? If the answer is not one sentence, the slide holds two ideas: split it. The signs: more than one chart, a chart and a table, bullets on two unrelated subjects, "also" between bullets.

## 4. Numbers instead of adjectives

| Vague | Concrete |
|---|---|
| "strong growth" | "+21% on the year" |
| "many customers" | "312 active customers, 28 more than in June" |
| "much faster" | "dispatch in 4 hours instead of 11" |

Without a number, a concrete case beats a vague claim: *"a 30-person restaurant group cut its stock by a third"* beats *"reduces stock"*. Name the source on the slide when the number carries weight.

## 5. Parallel bullets

Every bullet of a list has the same shape: the same opening form, the same length, the same level of abstraction. The eye finds the differences faster when the shape stays the same.

```markdown
- Move the Lyon route to the weekly truck
- Inspect returns within two days
- End the 2024 discount on the 40 slowest items
```

## 6. Six by six is a ceiling, not a target

At most six bullets per slide and about six words per bullet. Three bullets of four words usually beat six of six. At the ceiling, the slide does too much: split it. A deck read alone takes ten to twelve words per bullet, and never more than one line each.

## 7. Filler phrases

Cut them every time; write the sentence that comes after them instead:
- "In conclusion…" — the slide is visibly the last one.
- "As we saw…" — if the reader needs reminding, the earlier slide failed.
- "I would like to stress…" — stress it.
- "It is important to note…" — if it were not, you would not write it.
- "In other words…" — say it well the first time.
- "Obviously…" — if it is obvious, skip it; if not, the word talks down to the reader.

## 7b. Rhetorical constructions

Rule 7 bans phrases; this one bans sentence shapes, the ones that make a deck sound like a keynote instead of a professional document. They read as confident, so they survive review unless you look for them by name.

| Construction | Looks like | Write instead |
|---|---|---|
| Triad | "One warehouse, two shifts, zero errors." | "The warehouse ran two shifts in Q3 with no picking errors recorded." |
| Antithesis for effect | "We don't move boxes, we move trust." | "Customers renewed 94% of contracts in 2026." |
| Chiasmus | "The plan drives the numbers; the numbers drive the plan." | "The Q4 plan is revised every month against the actual orders." |
| Fragment for emphasis | "Next day. Every day." | "Orders placed by 6 pm are delivered the next working day." |
| Aphorism | "Speed is a habit." | "Dispatch time fell from 11 to 4 hours after the new picking routes." |
| Wordplay on the subject | "Delivering on our promise" | "We delivered 97% of orders on the promised day." |

The tell: a line that would work as a conference slide, or whose rhythm breaks if you remove a comma, is doing rhetorical work. Rewrite it as a plain declarative sentence.

It applies to every surface: titles, chapter and section subtitles, quotes used as takeaways, the cover's subtitle, the last slide. Subtitles are where it appears most, because a short line under a large title invites a flourish. The rule is the same for every audience, a board or investors included.

## 8. Sources on the slide

A number or a claim the argument rests on names its source on the same slide:
- in brackets: *"€1.4M (Northwind order system, Q3 2026)"*;
- as a footnote: `[^1]` and `[^1]: Northwind order system, Q3 2026` at the bottom of the slide;
- as a caption under a chart: *"Source: carrier invoices, Q4 2025 to Q3 2026"*.

Never only on a sources slide at the end: nobody goes back to it.

**A source the reader cannot open is half a source:**
- Every company named carries its website, and every claim that is not ours carries a reference the reader can follow.
- Write them as markdown links on the name: `[Rapido Freight](https://…)`. A bare address in the body is not clickable in the printed PDF.
- Open every link before writing it. A guessed link fails in front of the reader; when you cannot confirm one, name the company without a link.

## 9. Active voice

"We opened the Lyon depot in March", not "The Lyon depot was opened in March". The passive is fine when the actor does not matter or is unknown; in a business deck it almost always matters.

## 10. The cover title

The cover's `# ` title tells the audience the subject in two seconds:
- Strong: *"Q3 results for the Northwind board"*
- Weak: *"Quarterly update"* — no company, no quarter
- Weak: *"Beyond the numbers"* — a mood, not a subject (rule 7b)

Under it, one line that says what the deck is for: *"Revenue and costs against the Q3 plan, and the sales hire the board is asked to approve."* A statement of purpose, not a tagline.

## 11. Not their trade, and not our process

Two halves of one mistake: writing about the wrong subject.

**Do not explain the audience their own trade.** Their rules, their assets, their usual practice and their own vocabulary are not a lesson for them. It reads as condescension, and a technical reader is the one who will catch an explanation that is slightly wrong. The test: would this sentence be new to someone who does this job every day? If not, cut it. What lies outside their trade — a method from another field, a test protocol from another industry — is still explained, and §0 (e) still applies.

**Do not narrate our own process.** How long we searched, how many sources we read, what we got wrong first and corrected, that we checked the primary source, that we are being transparent: none of it is content. It is self-praise, or a confession the reader did not ask for. The one exception is §0 (f): "this is our estimate" tells the reader how much weight a number bears. One clause, inside the sentence with the number.

## 12. Which suppliers appear

**A supplier appears if it solves the reader's problem, whoever owns it, and the owner is named.** Ownership, size and independence are facts the reader weighs; they are not our grounds for hiding a working solution.

**A supplier left out because it closed, or because it does not solve the problem, does not appear**: not as a row, not as a footnote. The reader cannot act on it. What can be worth one line is the pattern the rejected ones show, such as *"none of the products that meet the specification is sold by an independent supplier"*; the list that produced it is not.

A criterion of ours is not a reason for the reader. "Bought by a large group" may be our selection rule while the reader buys from that group happily. When a supplier is excluded on our threshold rather than theirs, say so (§0 f).
